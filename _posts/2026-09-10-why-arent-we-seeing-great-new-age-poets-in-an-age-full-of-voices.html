Let us not even speak about Pothana, Vemana, Allasani Peddana and figures of that scale. That is a much bigger question. I am more curious about something closer to our own time.

Why do we not seem to find another Sri Sri or another Kaloji, especially in a world that still has so much pain, inequality, agitation, loneliness and social conflict?

It is not as if suffering has disappeared. If anything, we probably witness more of it now than people did in earlier generations. Through a phone screen, we see wars, protests, unemployment, discrimination, migration, personal tragedies and social tensions almost every day. The world's pain reaches us instantly. Yet somehow, I still feel that we are not producing voices that transform that pain into literature with the same force.

Maybe the reason is that seeing pain is not the same as understanding it.

Today, almost every event reaches us already surrounded by opinions. Before we have time to sit with something, there are reactions, arguments, hashtags, explanations and counter-explanations. We very quickly learn what different groups think about an issue. But poetry may require something slower...

It may require a person to stay with discomfort long enough to understand the human being beneath the event.

That, I feel, is where writers like Sri Sri and Kaloji were extraordinary. Their strength was not merely that they were angry about injustice or deeply affected by society. Many people are. Their strength was their ability to convert that feeling into language that went beyond the immediate moment. They could take something social and make it personal, or take something personal and make it universal.

Maybe that is also why great poetry is so rare. Anger itself is common. Pain is common. Even awareness is common. But transforming anger into thought, pain into language, and experience into something that another person can recognize as their own is a much more difficult act.

The internet generation is also far more aware of dignity, representation, identity and the consequences of language. I think much of that awareness is genuinely good. We understand many forms of hurt today that earlier generations may have ignored or normalized.

But increased awareness also changes how writers speak. People are more conscious of how a sentence can be interpreted, who may be hurt by it, and how quickly it can travel beyond its original context. This may make public writing more careful.

Carefulness is not necessarily a bad thing, but poetry sometimes grows from uncertainty, contradiction and discomfort. A writer may need room to think imperfectly before arriving at something meaningful. When every sentence can immediately become detached from its context and judged on its own, writers may naturally become more cautious.

Perhaps this is one reason anonymity has become interesting in modern art. Someone like Banksy can let the work speak before the public begins examining the person behind it. In a highly connected world, we often know so much about the creator that we start interpreting the work through the creator's identity, background or perceived position before allowing the work to stand on its own.

There is also another possibility. Maybe the next Sri Sri or Kaloji already exists, but we no longer recognize cultural figures in the same way.

Earlier, literature had time to travel slowly. A poem could move through newspapers, meetings, conversations and generations. Today, culture moves incredibly fast. A powerful line can become viral in the morning and disappear from public attention within days.

At the same time, people today often feel that they already know everything and that others know nothing. Social media gives everyone immediate access to information, opinions and platforms, but access to information is not the same as understanding. This confidence can become disruptive when people speak over others, dismiss experience, or treat every discussion as an opportunity to prove that they are right.

Instead of listening, learning and allowing ideas to develop, many conversations become competitions over who appears more informed. That environment makes it harder for thoughtful voices to emerge and be heard.

Literature needs attention, patience and the willingness to accept that we may not understand everything. When people are constantly trying to dominate conversations or display certainty, there is less space for reflection, doubt and the kind of deep listening from which meaningful writing often grows.

Visibility has increased, but permanence may have decreased.

This may also explain why we continue returning to older poets. Their words had time to settle into collective memory. We did not simply encounter them for a few seconds on a feed. We inherited them through books, classrooms, political movements, conversations and family memories. Their writing became part of the language itself.

Sometimes I also wonder whether we have made great rebels too comfortable by turning them into symbols. It is easy to quote Sri Sri. It is easy to post Kaloji on his birthday. It is far more difficult to retain the spirit of questioning that made them important in the first place.

Perhaps that is the paradox of all great writers... Society eventually celebrates people who once disturbed society.

So maybe the question is not simply, "Where is the next Sri Sri?" or "Where is the next Kaloji?" Maybe the more interesting question is whether the conditions that create and recognize such voices still exist in the same way.

The world certainly has enough pain or happiness or beauty to produce great poetry. It has enough injustice, love, loneliness, anger and hope. What may have changed is the way we experience all of it. We receive everything faster, react faster, judge faster and move on faster.

Poetry, on the other hand, may still require something ancient.

Time to observe. Time to feel. Time to doubt... and finally, the courage to put an uncomfortable truth into a few lines and leave it there for the world to think about.

Maybe another Sri Sri will come. Maybe another Kaloji already has. Perhaps we simply have to learn how to listen long enough to recognize them.
